Finally getting around to writing this up. You asked me last week at the
offsite what the deal was with the Cornerstone deal structure and why
finance has been so heads-down on it, and I promised I'd send something more
detailed than the two-minute version I gave you over coffee. Fair warning,
this got long. I'd rather over-explain than have you walk into the ops
review next month and get blindsided by the working capital impact.

Background first. As you know, Bridgepoint has been undercutting us on the
mid-market renewal segment for about three quarters now. Their list pricing
isn't actually lower than ours when you do the apples-to-apples comparison,
but the way they structure payment terms makes the effective cost
meaningfully lower for customers who care about cash flow timing, which is
most of them. Specifically, they've been offering 24-month deferred billing
on the services component with no interest, and they're eating the carry on
their balance sheet. We've lost at least four accounts I know of where the
procurement team explicitly told us the deciding factor was timing, not
price. Theo pulled the win/loss data in February and it was pretty stark.

So the strategy Finance and Sales leadership landed on, which got board
approval in April, has three pieces that work together. I'll go through them
in order because they only really make sense as a package.

First piece. We're partnering with Acumen Capital (a specialty finance firm,
they do this kind of thing for several B2B vendors) to offer customer
financing on the services component of any deal over 250k. The customer
signs a financing agreement with Acumen, not with us. Acumen pays us in full
at deal close, less a discount factor that runs about 4 to 6 percent
depending on customer credit. The customer then pays Acumen over 24 or 36
months. From the customer's perspective this looks almost identical to what
Bridgepoint is offering, but on our side we get full recognition of the cash
up
front and we move the credit risk off our balance sheet. The discount we pay
Acumen is meaningfully less than the cost of capital we'd incur if we tried
to carry the receivables ourselves, partly because Acumen is more efficient
at collections in this space and partly because of how they fund.

Second piece, and this is the one that's clever. Because Acumen is taking
the credit risk and we're getting paid up front, we can be much more
aggressive on the structure of the deal itself. So Sales is now empowered to
offer a "ramp" pricing model where year one is discounted (sometimes as deep
as 30 percent off list) with year two and three at a small premium to list.
The blended three-year value is comparable to or slightly better than our
standard pricing, but the customer gets a much lower year-one number, which
is what hits their budget and what procurement cares about. Bridgepoint
can't
easily match this because their financing is internal, so deeper year-one
discounting actually hurts their cash position. We're essentially using
Acumen's balance sheet to fund a pricing posture our competitor structurally
can't replicate, at least not without changing how they fund the business.

Third piece. We're using the up-front cash from the Acumen deals to fund a
customer success investment program (Tobias is running it out of the
post-sale org) that's specifically designed to drive expansion in years two
and three. The thinking is that the ramp pricing creates a natural moment in
year two where the customer is paying more, and historically that's when
churn risk spikes. So we're front-loading the relationship investment to get
ahead of that. The expected expansion uplift, if we hit even half of what
the model assumes, more than pays for the discount factor we're giving up to
Acumen.

Now, the accounting on this is where it gets complicated and where my team
has been spending most of our time. A few things worth knowing.

Revenue recognition under ASC 606 is largely unaffected because we're
recognizing based on performance obligations, not cash receipt, but the
contract modification and variable consideration analysis on the ramp deals
is non-trivial. We're treating the ramp as a single contract with the
discount allocated proportionally across the performance obligations, which
means year one revenue recognition is actually higher than year one
billings. That's going to create a deferred revenue / contract asset dynamic
on the balance sheet that's going to look weird if you're not expecting it.
I've been telling people to look at billings and ARR if they want to
understand the business, and treat GAAP revenue as a lagging indicator for
the next several quarters.

The Acumen relationship gets accounted for as a sale of receivables, not a
financing, because it's true sale treatment with no recourse. That was a
long conversation with the auditors and I will spare you the details, but
the upshot is the discount factor flows through as a contra-revenue item,
not as interest expense. So our gross margin will look slightly worse on
these deals even though the economics are good. Again, something to be aware
of when you're reading the financials.

The cash flow statement is going to look great, possibly suspiciously great,
for the next two or three quarters as we ramp this up, because we're
effectively pulling forward cash that would have been collected over 24
months. I've already flagged this to IR so they can manage expectations, but
I mention it because someone in your org may notice the cash flow vs.
earnings divergence and ask questions. The short answer is it's real cash,
it's not a one-time benefit in the sense of being non-recurring (we expect
the program to keep running), but the year-over-year growth rate in
operating cash flow is going to be inflated until we lap the program launch.

There's also a tax angle that I won't get into deeply but the gist is that
the up-front recognition for tax purposes creates a timing difference we
have to manage, and we're working with KPMG on the structure. Don't worry
about it from your end, just know it's been thought about.

Risks. Two main ones. The first is that Acumen's underwriting standards may
screen out some customers we'd otherwise want to do business with, in which
case we either lose the deal or we offer them our standard terms and lose
the pricing advantage. We've negotiated a fallback where Acumen will work
with us on borderline credits at a higher discount, but there's a tail of
customers that just won't qualify. The second risk is concentration. We're
putting a meaningful portion of our customer financing through one
counterparty, and if Acumen ever has issues we'd have a real problem. We're
starting conversations with a second provider for diversification but that's
six months out at least.

Last thing. The reason I'm telling you all this in detail is that your team
is going to start seeing some operational consequences, specifically around
how deals get booked in the CRM, what shows up in the order management
